MARITIME SAFETY: PAST AND PRESENT CHALLENGES

From Traditional Navigation Risks to Modern Warfare and Global Maritime Security

1. INTRODUCTION

Maritime safety has evolved significantly over the past century. In the early days of commercial shipping, safety measures were often introduced in response to major accidents and loss of life. Today, international conventions, advanced navigation systems, and improved emergency procedures have made shipping considerably safer.

However, modern shipping faces challenges that extend beyond traditional accidents. Geopolitical conflicts, attacks on commercial vessels, cyber threats, climate change, human fatigue, and the introduction of alternative marine fuels have created a more complex maritime environment.

The ongoing conflicts affecting the Middle East, the Red Sea, and the Black Sea demonstrate that merchant shipping is increasingly exposed to risks beyond the control of individual vessels and their crews. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has repeatedly warned that attacks on merchant ships endanger civilian seafarers and disrupt international trade. 

Maritime safety is no longer just about preventing accidents at sea; it is also about protecting human life in an increasingly unpredictable world.

2. PAST CHALLENGES: THE EARLY ERA OF MARITIME SAFETY

In the past, maritime safety was largely reactive, with important improvements often following major accidents.

A. Lack of International Safety Standards

Before the development of comprehensive international maritime regulations, safety requirements varied considerably between countries and shipping companies. The sinking of the Titanic in 1912 exposed serious shortcomings in lifeboat capacity, emergency preparedness, and radio communications. These events contributed to the adoption of the first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) in 1914.

B. Limitations in Navigation Technology

Mariners relied heavily on celestial navigation, compasses, charts, and visual observations. The absence of modern radar, satellite navigation, and electronic chart systems made it more difficult to detect hazards, avoid collisions, and determine a vessel's position accurately.

C. Ship Design and Fire Safety

Earlier vessels often had less sophisticated fire-detection systems, limited firefighting arrangements, and fewer advanced methods of compartmentalisation. Major casualties, including the Morro Castle disaster in 1934, highlighted the need for stronger fire prevention and emergency response measures.

D. Human Factors and Training

Formalised international training and certification standards were less developed. Fatigue, inadequate supervision, poor communication, and inconsistent emergency training contributed to maritime accidents.

The adoption of the International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers (STCW) in 1978 marked a major step towards establishing internationally recognised training and competency requirements.

The lesson from the past: Maritime safety improved when accidents were investigated, weaknesses were identified, and regulations were strengthened.

3. PRESENT CHALLENGES: THE MARITIME INDUSTRY IN 2026

Modern shipping operates under an extensive regulatory framework that includes SOLAS, MARPOL, STCW, the ISM Code, the ISPS Code, and the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006.

Nevertheless, the existence of regulations does not eliminate every risk. The challenges of modern shipping increasingly involve the interaction of human performance, advanced technology, commercial interests, environmental responsibilities, and geopolitical instability.

A. War, Geopolitical Conflicts, and Maritime Security

One of the most serious contemporary challenges is the impact of armed conflicts on international shipping.

The Strait of Hormuz, the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, and the Black Sea have demonstrated how regional conflicts can threaten merchant vessels and civilian crews.
